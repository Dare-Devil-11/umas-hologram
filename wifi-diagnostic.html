Signal & Environment

✓
Check RSSI signal strength
Hold Option + click WiFi icon in menu bar. RSSI worse than −70 dBm = weak. Ideal: −50 or better.
/System/Library/PrivateFrameworks/Apple80211.framework/Versions/Current/Resources/airport -I | grep -E "SSID|RSSI|noise|channel"
How to read your signal strength
1
Open Terminal (press Cmd+Space, type "Terminal", press Enter)
Terminal is where you run diagnostic commands. It looks like a black or dark window with a text prompt.
2
Copy the command above and paste it into Terminal, press Enter
You'll see a few lines of output with numbers.
3
Find the line that says "agrCtlRSSI" — that's your signal
Read the number after it:
What the number means
-30 to -50Excellent — you're close to the router
-51 to -70Acceptable — some drops possible
-71 or worseWeak — this is causing your drops. Move closer.
4
Also check "agrCtlNoise" — lower is better
Noise below -85 = good. Above -70 = heavy interference from microwaves, baby monitors, or neighbors. If signal is OK but noise is high → interference is your problem.
5
If signal is weak: mark as problem and move to signal fixes
Move the router higher, away from walls. Remove objects between you and it. Or use a WiFi extender / mesh node.
✓
Scan for channel congestion
Many networks on your channel = interference = drops. Look for crowded channels and switch your router.
/System/Library/PrivateFrameworks/Apple80211.framework/Versions/Current/Resources/airport -s
How to find and fix channel congestion
1
Run the command above in Terminal
You'll see a list of all nearby WiFi networks. Each row has: network name, signal (RSSI), channel number.
2
Find your network in the list. Look at its channel number.
Then count how many OTHER networks are on the same channel. More than 3 networks on the same channel = congested.
3
For 2.4 GHz: only channels 1, 6, and 11 don't overlap. Pick the emptiest one.
For 5 GHz: channels 36, 40, 44, 48 are usually less crowded. Try any that has fewer neighbors.
4
Log into your router admin panel to change the channel
Open a browser and go to 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1 (your gateway). Login is usually admin/admin or printed on the router label. Find Wireless Settings → Channel → change it.
5
After changing: reconnect your device and monitor for 15 minutes
If drops stop → congestion was your problem. Mark this resolved.
✓
Distance test — move 5 ft from router
Move close to the router and wait 10 min. If drops stop → signal/distance is the problem.
Distance elimination test
1
Bring your laptop to within 5 feet of your router
Don't unplug anything — just physically move with your device.
2
Open a streaming video (YouTube, Netflix) and let it play for 10 minutes
This generates real traffic. If it stays connected without a single drop, your router is fine — you just need better signal where you normally work.
3
Interpret the result:
Test Result
No drops up closeSignal/distance = problem. Fix below.
Still drops up closeSignal is NOT the issue → check router/ISP sections
4
If signal IS the problem — fix options:
• Move router to a central location, elevated, away from walls
• Add a WiFi extender ($25–50) midway between router and your work area
• Upgrade to a mesh system (Eero, TP-Link Deco) for whole-home coverage
✓
Check 2.4 GHz vs 5 GHz band
5 GHz = faster but shorter range. Far from router on 5 GHz = drops. Switch to 2.4 GHz.
networksetup -getinfo Wi-Fi
How to check and switch your WiFi band
1
Hold Option and click the WiFi icon in your Mac menu bar
You'll see your connected network name followed by a frequency like "5 GHz" or "2.4 GHz".
2
Understand the tradeoff:
Band comparison
2.4 GHzSlower, but reaches further through walls
5 GHzFaster, but signal dies past 30 feet or one wall
3
If you're more than 20 feet away or through multiple walls: switch to 2.4 GHz
Most routers broadcast both under different names (e.g., "MyWiFi" and "MyWiFi_5G"). In your Mac WiFi menu, forget the 5G network and connect to the regular one.
4
If your router uses one name for both: log in to router admin and enable "Band Steering" — or split them into two separate names
Go to 192.168.1.1 → Wireless → Advanced → Enable separate SSIDs for 2.4GHz and 5GHz.
✓
Run macOS Wireless Diagnostics
Built-in Apple tool. Run it WHILE drops happen — logs the exact disconnect moment.
open /System/Library/CoreServices/Applications/Wireless\ Diagnostics.app
Using Apple Wireless Diagnostics
1
Run the command above to open Wireless Diagnostics
Or: hold Option + click WiFi icon in menu bar → Open Wireless Diagnostics.
2
DO NOT click Continue yet — first, go to the Window menu in the menu bar
Choose "Monitor" from the Window menu. This starts live logging of your WiFi quality.
3
Leave the Monitor window open and use your computer normally
When a drop happens, you'll see the signal graph dip to zero or a disconnect event logged.
4
After a drop: go back, look at the "Summary" tab and note the time of disconnect
The tool shows exactly what channel, RSSI, and noise level were at the moment of the drop.
5
To save a full report: File → Save Diagnostics → save to Desktop
Send this .diag file to Apple Support or share with your ISP as evidence of the problem.

Router & Modem
0/4
✓
Ping your router gateway
Drops to the router = problem between you and router, not the ISP. Packet loss >2% = WiFi issue.
ping -c 30 $(netstat -rn | grep default | head -1 | awk '{print $2}')
How to read your gateway ping results
1
Paste and run the command in Terminal. Wait ~30 seconds for it to finish.
It pings your router 30 times. The last 2 lines are what matter.
2
Look for the final summary line — it says "packet loss"
Packet loss thresholds
0% lossRouter connection is stable
1–5% lossMarginal — WiFi signal or interference
>5% lossMajor WiFi problem between you and router
3
Also look at the "round-trip" time (ms)
Under 5ms to your router is normal. Over 50ms = the router itself is struggling (overloaded or failing).
4
If loss is high: this is a WiFi/router issue, not ISP — try power cycling the router first
Unplug the router for 30 seconds, plug back in, wait 60 seconds, then retest.
✓
Ping 8.8.8.8 — bypass DNS
Router pings fine but 8.8.8.8 drops = problem between router and ISP. Check modem/coax line.
ping -c 60 -i 1 8.8.8.8 | tail -5
How to isolate router vs ISP problem
1
Run the gateway ping test first (item above). Note the result.
This establishes whether the router itself is dropping you.
2
Run this 8.8.8.8 ping command. Wait ~60 seconds.
8.8.8.8 is Google's server. This bypasses DNS completely — just raw IP routing.
3
Compare both results to isolate where the problem is:
Comparison matrix
Both OKRouter + ISP fine → check DNS or Mac
GW drop, 8.8.8.8 OKWiFi or router issue
GW OK, 8.8.8.8 dropISP or modem problem
Both dropRouter overloaded or failing
4
If ISP is indicated: call them with the ping log as evidence
Say: "I have 0% loss to my router but X% loss to 8.8.8.8 — can you check my line signal levels?"
✓
Power cycle modem + router
Fixes memory leaks and stuck DHCP leases. Unplug modem first, then router. Wait 30 sec.
arp -a
Correct power cycle sequence
1
Unplug the MODEM first (the box that connects to the wall/cable)
Wait 30 full seconds. This forces the modem to drop its session with your ISP.
2
While the modem is still unplugged, unplug the ROUTER too
Wait 10 more seconds. This clears the router's memory and DHCP lease table.
3
Plug the MODEM back in first. Wait 60 seconds until its lights stabilize.
Most modems have 3–4 lights. Wait until they're all solid (not blinking rapidly).
4
Plug the ROUTER back in. Wait 60 seconds.
Reconnect your Mac to WiFi. Test for 15 minutes. If drops return within a few hours → your router has a memory leak and needs a scheduled nightly reboot (check router admin settings).
✓
Check for IP address conflict
Two devices with same IP = one drops randomly. Compare your IP to ARP table.
ipconfig getifaddr en0 && echo "---ARP Table---" && arp -a | grep -v incomplete
How to detect and fix an IP conflict
1
Run the command. The first line shows YOUR IP address (e.g., 192.168.1.45)
Write it down.
2
Look through the ARP table lines below it for your IP
Each line looks like: ? (192.168.1.45) at aa:bb:cc:dd:ee:ff — if you see your IP listed twice with DIFFERENT MAC addresses, you have a conflict.
3
If conflict found: force a new IP by renewing DHCP
sudo ipconfig set en0 DHCP
This requests a fresh IP from the router.
4
Long-term fix: set a DHCP reservation in your router for your Mac
Log into router admin → find DHCP settings → add a "static lease" for your Mac's MAC address. This locks your Mac to one IP forever and prevents future conflicts.

DNS

✓
Compare DNS speed — ISP vs Cloudflare
Slow DNS makes every page load pause even when bandwidth is fine. Under 50ms = good.
time dig google.com @8.8.8.8 +short; time dig google.com @1.1.1.1 +short
How to read DNS speed test results
1
Run the command in Terminal. It will run two tests back to back.
You'll see an IP address, then a time result like "real 0m0.045s" — that 0.045 is 45 milliseconds.
2
Look at the "real" time for each test
DNS response times
Under 50msFast — DNS is not your problem
50–200msSlow — consider switching DNS
Over 200ms or timeoutDNS is causing your "drops" — switch now
3
If Cloudflare (1.1.1.1) is faster — switch to it using the command in the next item
DNS failures feel exactly like internet drops because websites can't be found even though the connection is live.
✓
Flush DNS cache
Stale cache causes pages to fail even when internet is live. Run this and retest immediately.
sudo dscacheutil -flushcache; sudo killall -HUP mDNSResponder; echo "DNS flushed"
How to flush DNS and test if it fixed things
1
Paste the command into Terminal and press Enter
It will ask for your Mac password (for sudo). Type it — nothing shows while you type, that's normal.
2
You should see "DNS flushed" printed. That means it worked.
The DNS cache is now cleared. Any previously broken lookups will be resolved fresh.
3
Immediately open a browser and try loading 3 different websites
If they all load cleanly now → stale DNS was causing your "drops". It looked like the internet died but it was just bad cached lookups.
4
If the problem keeps coming back: switch DNS servers permanently (next item)
A one-time flush is a temporary fix. Switching to Cloudflare DNS is the permanent solution.
✓
Switch to Cloudflare DNS 1.1.1.1
Takes effect immediately. Fastest fix if DNS is the issue. No reboot needed.
networksetup -setdnsservers Wi-Fi 1.1.1.1 8.8.8.8 && echo "DNS updated"
Switching DNS — step by step
1
Run the command. You should see "DNS updated" printed.
This sets Cloudflare (1.1.1.1) as primary and Google (8.8.8.8) as backup — both are faster and more reliable than most ISP DNS servers.
2
Verify it worked:
networksetup -getdnsservers Wi-Fi
Should print: 1.1.1.1 then 8.8.8.8
3
Test: open a browser and load 5 different sites
If everything loads smoothly now — DNS was your issue.
4
To undo (revert to ISP DNS):
networksetup -setdnsservers Wi-Fi "Empty"
This removes the override and lets your router assign DNS again automatically.

Run networkQuality test
Built-in macOS Monterey+ tool. Shows upload + download quality and responsiveness score.
networkQuality
Reading networkQuality results
1
Run "networkQuality" in Terminal. Wait ~15 seconds for it to complete.
It tests your real upload + download quality — not just speed, but consistency.
2
Look at the "Responsiveness" score (RPM)
Responsiveness (RPM) thresholds
Above 20,000 RPMExcellent — low latency, no buffering
5,000–20,000 RPMModerate — video calls may stutter
Under 5,000 RPMPoor — connection is unstable under load
3
Run it twice: once alone, once while streaming video
If RPM drops significantly under load → your connection can't handle concurrent traffic. This points to ISP throttling or a router that's overloaded.
✓
Check en0 interface errors
Non-zero ierr/oerr = driver or hardware fault on the WiFi card. Numbers going up = packets dropping.
netstat -I en0 2 | head -10 && ifconfig en0 | grep -E "status|errors"
Reading interface error stats
1
Run the command. The netstat output shows a table — focus on the columns labeled "Ierrs" and "Oerrs"
Ierrs = input errors (packets your Mac dropped receiving). Oerrs = output errors (packets failed to send).
2
Interpret the error counts:
Error interpretation
All zerosWiFi hardware is working fine
Non-zero, not growingOld errors — probably not active issue
Numbers growingActive driver or hardware fault
3
To watch if errors grow: run it twice, 30 seconds apart
netstat -I en0 1 5
Shows 5 readings, one per second. If Ierrs or Oerrs column increases = active error condition.
4
If errors are growing: try turning WiFi off and back on
System Settings → Wi-Fi → toggle off, wait 5 seconds, toggle on. If errors reset to zero and stop growing → driver needed a reset, not hardware fault.
✓
Check preferred networks list
Mac auto-jumps to nearby known networks — looks like a drop. Remove old unused networks.
networksetup -listpreferredwirelessnetworks en0
Clean up your saved networks list
1
Run the command to see all networks your Mac remembers
Your Mac will auto-connect to ANY network on this list if it's nearby — even coffee shops, hotels, old employers, or a neighbor's open network.
2
Remove networks you no longer use via command line:
networksetup -removepreferredwirelessnetwork en0 "NetworkNameHere"
Replace NetworkNameHere with the exact name from the list.
3
Or use the GUI: System Settings → Wi-Fi → scroll down → tap "..." next to old networks → Forget
Keep only your home network and any networks you actively use.
4
After cleanup: check "Ask to join networks" is OFF in Wi-Fi settings
This prevents your Mac from showing popups or joining random open networks.
✓
Force renew DHCP lease
Expired DHCP = your IP changes mid-session = drops. This renews without full disconnect.
sudo ipconfig set en0 DHCP && echo "Lease renewed"
DHCP lease renewal walkthrough
1
Check your current IP and lease time:
ipconfig getpacket en0 | grep -E "lease|ip_address|subnet"
Look for "lease_time" — if it's 0 or very low, your lease has expired or is expiring soon.
2
Run the renewal command. Enter your Mac password when prompted.
You'll see a brief 1–2 second WiFi reconnect. This is normal — your Mac is re-requesting its IP address.
3
Verify you got a new (or confirmed) IP:
ipconfig getifaddr en0
The IP should be something like 192.168.x.x. If it says 169.254.x.x — that's a DHCP failure (your router didn't respond). Power cycle the router.
4
169.254.x.x address means self-assigned IP — the router DHCP server is not responding
This is a router problem. Power cycle modem + router (see Router section). If it persists after reboot — the router's DHCP service may be full or broken. Log in to router admin and check DHCP pool.

ISP / Line
0/3
✓
Traceroute — find which hop drops
Hop 1 = your router. Hop 2–3 timing out = ISP problem, not yours. Screenshot for ISP call.
traceroute -n 8.8.8.8
How to read a traceroute
1
Run the command. It takes 30–60 seconds and prints results line by line.
Each line is one "hop" — a router your data passes through on the way to 8.8.8.8 (Google).
2
Find the first hop — that's your router. It should respond in under 5ms.
Lines look like: "1 192.168.1.1 1.2ms 1.1ms 1.4ms". Three numbers = three probe times.
3
Look for lines with "* * *" — those are timeouts
Where the problem is
Hop 1 = * * *Router unreachable — WiFi/router issue
Hop 2–3 = * * *ISP gateway dropping — call your ISP
Hop 5+ = * * *Normal — those routers block traceroute. Not your problem.
4
Screenshot the full output and save it
When you call your ISP, share this screenshot. Say: "Hops 1–2 respond fine but hop 3 times out — that's on your network, not mine."
✓
Log drops for 5 minutes — build evidence
300 pings saved to Desktop. ISP cannot argue with a packet loss log file.
ping -c 300 -i 1 8.8.8.8 | tee ~/Desktop/ping-log.txt; tail -3 ~/Desktop/ping-log.txt
Building an ISP evidence log
1
Run the command and leave it running for 5 minutes
It pings Google once per second for 300 seconds. You'll see "bytes from 8.8.8.8" for each successful ping. Missing lines = dropped packets.
2
After it finishes, look at the last 3 lines it prints
The summary shows: packets transmitted, received, packet loss %, and round-trip stats.
3
The full log is saved at ~/Desktop/ping-log.txt — open it to see the exact timestamps of every drop
Lines with "Request timeout" are your drops. The line number roughly equals the second into the test.
4
Use this when calling ISP support:
"I ran 300 pings to 8.8.8.8 and got X% packet loss. The log is timestamped. This is coming from outside my network — I need a line quality check."

ISPs will send a technician when you have logs. Without logs, they'll say "it looks fine on our end."
✓
Test wired ethernet — definitive split test
Drops stop on ethernet = WiFi hardware issue. Drops continue on ethernet = ISP/modem problem.
Ethernet isolation test — the definitive test
1
Get an ethernet cable and plug one end directly into your router, the other into your Mac
If your Mac doesn't have an ethernet port, use a USB-C to ethernet adapter ($15–20 on Amazon). This is worth buying just for diagnosis.
2
Turn WiFi OFF on your Mac
System Settings → Wi-Fi → toggle off. This forces all traffic through the ethernet cable only.
3
Use your connection normally for 15–30 minutes. Stream video, browse, run the ping log.
This is your control test.
4
Read the result:
What it means
No drops on ethernetWiFi hardware or signal = your problem. Fix the WiFi.
Still drops on ethernetModem or ISP = your problem. Call ISP with your ping log.
5
This test splits the problem space in half — the result tells you exactly where to focus
There is no faster way to isolate WiFi vs. ISP than this physical test.
